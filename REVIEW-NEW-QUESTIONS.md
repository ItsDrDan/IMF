## Ion–dipole

CHEM 1A.

### 1. Which ion attracts the water molecules around it more strongly: Na⁺ or Mg²⁺?

- **✓ Mg²⁺**: Its 2+ charge (on a smaller ion) pulls on water’s δ− oxygen much harder than the 1+ charge of Na⁺.
- Na⁺: Na⁺ has the smaller charge, so its pull on a water dipole is weaker.
- They attract water equally: The strength of an ion–dipole attraction depends on the charge of the ion: 2+ pulls harder than 1+.

Hint: Compare the charges. A bigger charge pulls harder on the δ− end of water.

### 2. Which pair is held together by an ion–dipole attraction?

- **✓ K⁺ and H₂O**: An ion (K⁺) and a polar molecule (H₂O): ion–dipole.
- H₂O and H₂O: Two water molecules attract by hydrogen bonding. Neither one is an ion.
- Na⁺ and Cl⁻ in a salt crystal: Two ions attract by ionic bonding. Ion–dipole needs a polar molecule as the partner.
- CH₄ and CH₄: Two nonpolar molecules attract by dispersion forces only.

Hint: Ion–dipole needs one ion and one polar molecule.

## Sugars, starch and cellulose

CHEM 3B.

### 3. Table sugar (sucrose) is a molecular compound, yet it is a hard solid at room temperature. What mainly holds one sucrose molecule to the next in the crystal?

- **✓ Hydrogen bonds between –OH groups on neighboring molecules**: Sucrose has eight O–H groups, so each molecule hydrogen bonds to several neighbors.
- Ionic bonds: Sucrose is molecular. It has no ions.
- Covalent bonds between the molecules: The molecules are not bonded to each other: they separate as whole molecules when sugar dissolves.
- Dispersion forces only: It has dispersion forces too, but with eight O–H groups the hydrogen bonds matter most.

### 4. A cotton towel is mostly cellulose. Why does cotton soak up water so well?

- **✓ The –OH groups on cellulose hydrogen bond with water**: Water clings to the many –OH groups on the fibers. The fibers themselves stay solid, because the chains are also hydrogen bonded to each other.
- Cellulose dissolves in water: Cotton does not dissolve: its chains hold on to each other. Water sticks to the –OH groups on the surface of the fibers.
- Cellulose is nonpolar: A nonpolar fiber would repel water. Cellulose is covered in polar –OH groups.
- Water breaks the covalent bonds in cellulose: Wetting a towel is a physical change. No covalent bond breaks.

## Surface tension and viscosity

CHEM 3A and 1A.

### 5. Rain beads up on a freshly waxed car instead of spreading out. Why?

- **✓ Water molecules attract each other much more than they attract the nonpolar wax**: Hydrogen bonds pull the water inward into a bead. That inward pull is surface tension.
- Wax is polar and pushes the water away: Wax is nonpolar. Water simply attracts other water molecules more than it attracts wax.
- Water has a low surface tension: It is the reverse: water’s high surface tension is what pulls it into beads.
- The water reacts with the wax: Nothing reacts. Beading is a physical effect of the attractions between water molecules.

### 6. Motor oil and gasoline are both mixtures of nonpolar hydrocarbons, but motor oil pours much more slowly. Why?

- **✓ Oil molecules are much longer, so their dispersion forces are stronger and the chains tangle**: Gasoline molecules have about 5 to 12 carbons. Motor oil molecules have 20 or more, so a higher molar mass and stronger dispersion forces: a higher viscosity.
- Motor oil can hydrogen bond: Hydrocarbons have every H on carbon. The H must be bonded directly to N, O or F. An H on carbon (or on Cl, S, P) does not hydrogen bond.
- Motor oil is polar: Both are nonpolar hydrocarbons.
- Gasoline molecules are the bigger ones: Gasoline molecules are the smaller ones, which is why gasoline also evaporates faster.

## Osmosis basics

All three courses.

### 7. A semipermeable membrane separates 0.10 M glucose (left) from 0.30 M glucose (right). Which way is the net flow of water?

- **✓ From left to right, toward the 0.30 M side**: Water moves toward the higher solute concentration, which dilutes it.
- From right to left, toward the 0.10 M side: Water moves toward the side with more solute (where water is less concentrated). A hypertonic bath pulls water out and shrinks the cell; a hypotonic bath sends water in.
- No net flow: That happens only when the two concentrations are equal. Here they are 0.10 M and 0.30 M.
- Glucose moves to the left instead: The membrane blocks the glucose. Only the water can cross.

### 8. Sugar is sprinkled on sliced strawberries. In a few minutes they sit in a red syrup. Where did the water come from?

- **✓ Out of the strawberry cells, by osmosis**: The sugar makes a very concentrated solution on the surface. Water leaves the cells toward the higher solute concentration.
- Out of the air: The water comes from inside the fruit. It moves toward the sugar by osmosis.
- The sugar melted: Sugar melts far above room temperature. It dissolved in water drawn out of the cells.
- Sugar reacted with the fruit to make water: Nothing reacts. Water moved out of the cells toward the higher solute concentration.

### 9. A slice of raw potato is left in very salty water. An hour later it is limp. Why?

- **✓ Water left the potato cells, because the salt water is hypertonic**: Water moves toward the higher solute concentration outside. The cells lose water and go soft.
- Water entered the potato cells: Water entering would make the slice firm. Water moves toward the side with more solute (where water is less concentrated). A hypertonic bath pulls water out and shrinks the cell; a hypotonic bath sends water in.
- Salt filled the cells and dissolved them: The main effect is water leaving. The membrane lets water through far more easily than ions.
- The salt water is hypotonic: Very salty water has more solute than the cells, so it is hypertonic.

## Protein folding

CHEM 3B.

### 10. Which bond joins one amino acid to the next along a protein chain?

- **✓ A peptide bond, which is covalent**: The C=O carbon of one amino acid is bonded to the N of the next. This is the primary structure, and it survives heating.
- A hydrogen bond: Hydrogen bonds hold the folded shape. They do not join the amino acids into a chain. A hydrogen bond is an attraction between molecules, with no shared electrons. It is much weaker than a covalent bond.
- A disulfide bond: Disulfide bonds link two cysteine side chains. The backbone is joined by peptide bonds.
- A salt bridge: A salt bridge is an attraction between charged side chains. The backbone is joined by covalent peptide bonds.

### 11. A permanent wave changes the shape of hair by breaking and re-forming the links between cysteine side chains. Which links are those?

- **✓ Disulfide bonds**: A disulfide bond (–S–S–) is a real covalent bond between two cysteines. The other side-chain interactions are attractions, not shared electrons. Because they are covalent, the new shape lasts.
- Hydrogen bonds: Water alone rearranges hydrogen bonds, which is why wet-set hair goes flat again. A permanent wave changes the covalent –S–S– links.
- Peptide bonds: Breaking peptide bonds would cut the protein chains apart. A perm changes only the links between cysteines.
- Salt bridges: Cysteine side chains are not charged. Two cysteines link through sulfur: –S–S–.

### 12. In a β-pleated sheet, the hydrogen bonds run between…

- **✓ backbone C=O and N–H groups on neighboring strands**: The strands lie side by side, and each C=O points at an N–H on the strand next to it.
- side chains on neighboring strands: Secondary structure uses only the backbone (C=O and N–H hydrogen bonds). Tertiary structure comes from the side chains.
- sulfur atoms: Sulfur–sulfur links are disulfide bonds, which are covalent and belong to tertiary structure.
- C–H groups: The H must be bonded directly to N, O or F. An H on carbon (or on Cl, S, P) does not hydrogen bond.

## Membranes, soaps and colloids

CHEM 3B.

### 13. Soap molecules gather into a ball called a micelle in water. Where are the nonpolar tails?

- **✓ Pointing inward, packed together away from the water**: The tails attract each other by dispersion forces. The charged heads cover the outside and face the water.
- Pointing outward into the water: The nonpolar tails are hydrophobic. The charged heads are the part that faces water.
- Half in and half out: Every soap molecule turns the same way: tail in, head out.
- Dissolved one by one in the water: A nonpolar tail does not dissolve in water. That is why the molecules gather into micelles.

### 14. Oil and vinegar dressing separates into layers on standing. Mayonnaise is also oil and vinegar, yet it stays mixed. Why?

- **✓ Egg yolk supplies an emulsifier that coats each oil droplet**: Lecithin from the yolk has a hydrophobic end in the oil and a hydrophilic end in the water, so the droplets cannot merge.
- The oil in mayonnaise is polar: It is the same nonpolar oil. The difference is the emulsifier around each droplet.
- Mayonnaise is a true solution: Its oil droplets are far bigger than single molecules. Mayonnaise is a colloid (an emulsion).
- Mayonnaise is kept cold: Temperature is not what keeps it mixed. The emulsifier is.

### 15. Fats and cholesterol travel through the blood, which is mostly water, inside particles called lipoproteins. What must the outside of a lipoprotein be like?

- **✓ Polar or charged, with the nonpolar lipids hidden inside**: Phospholipid heads and proteins face the water. The fats and cholesterol stay in the nonpolar middle.
- Nonpolar, like the fats inside: A nonpolar surface would not stay spread out in water. The particles would clump together.
- Covalently bonded to water: Nothing bonds covalently to the water. The surface attracts water through its polar and charged groups.
- It makes no difference: Like dissolves like: only a polar or charged surface stays mixed with water.

## Dispersion, size and shape

All three courses (opened to CHEM 3A and 3B in v18).

### 16. Where do dispersion forces come from?

- **✓ Temporary dipoles, made when the electrons in a molecule are unevenly spread for an instant**: A temporary dipole in one molecule pulls the electrons of its neighbor out of place, and the two attract.
- Permanent dipoles in polar molecules: That is dipole–dipole attraction. Dispersion forces act even between nonpolar molecules and single atoms.
- Full charges on ions: That is ionic bonding or ion–dipole attraction. Dispersion needs no ions.
- An H bonded to N, O or F: That is hydrogen bonding. Dispersion forces need only electrons.

### 17. Which substances have dispersion forces?

- **✓ All of them: every atom and molecule has electrons**: Every molecule has dispersion forces, because every molecule has electrons. Polar molecules have them too.
- Only nonpolar molecules: For nonpolar molecules dispersion is the only force. Polar molecules have it too, along with their other forces.
- Only polar molecules: Nonpolar molecules and noble gas atoms have dispersion forces. For them it is the only force.
- Only large molecules: Large molecules have stronger dispersion forces, but even helium has some: it liquefies at 4.2 K.

### 18. Hexane and 2,2-dimethylbutane are both C₆H₁₄. Hexane boils at 69 °C and 2,2-dimethylbutane at 50 °C. Why?

- **✓ Hexane is a long chain, so its molecules touch over more surface**: Same formula and the same 50 electrons. The branched isomer is more compact, so its molecules touch less and its dispersion forces are weaker.
- Hexane has more electrons: They are isomers: the same formula and the same 50 electrons.
- Hexane is polar: Both are nonpolar hydrocarbons.
- Hexane has stronger C–H bonds: Boiling pulls whole molecules apart from each other. No covalent bond breaks: the steam is still H₂O.

## Fatty acids: melting points

### 19. Arachidonic acid (20:4) has a longer chain than stearic acid (18:0). Yet it melts at −49 °C, and stearic acid melts at 69 °C. Why?

- A longer chain always lowers the melting point: A longer chain raises the melting point when everything else is the same. Here the four cis double bonds matter more.
- **✓ Its four cis double bonds kink the chain, so the molecules cannot pack closely**: Two extra carbons add a little dispersion, but four kinks keep the chains far apart. Loose packing means weak dispersion forces and a low melting point.
- Its covalent bonds are weaker: Melting separates molecules from each other. No covalent bond breaks.
- Arachidonic acid has no dispersion forces: Every molecule has dispersion forces, because every molecule has electrons. Polar molecules have them too.

Hint: Compare the double bonds: 0 against 4. What does each cis double bond do to the shape of the chain?

## Lab predictions: Energy Well

### 20. Where on the energy curve do two molecules have the lowest potential energy?

- **✓ At the bottom of the well, a small distance apart**: There the attraction and the repulsion balance. That is the most stable separation.
- Pushed as close together as possible: Too close, and the two electron clouds repel: the curve shoots up on the left.
- Very far apart: Far apart there is no attraction, so the energy is higher than at the bottom of the well.

In the lab afterward: Lower the kinetic energy all the way and see where the pair settles.

### 21. Two molecules flew apart because their kinetic energy was above the top of the well. The sample is then cooled. What can happen?

- **✓ They can be held together again, once the kinetic energy drops below the depth of the well**: Slower molecules cannot escape the attraction. This is what happens when a gas condenses.
- They stay apart for good: The attraction is still there. Cool the molecules enough and it can hold them again.
- They join by a covalent bond: This well is an attraction between whole molecules. No electrons are shared.

In the lab afterward: Raise the kinetic energy until the pair separates, then lower it again and watch.

## Lab predictions: Electron Coin-Flip

### 22. Neon has 10 electrons and argon has 18. Which atom is caught more often with its electrons split unevenly?

- **✓ Argon**: An exact even split gets rarer as electrons are added: about 25% of the time for neon and 19% for argon. So argon is lopsided more often, which fits its higher boiling point (87 K vs 27 K).
- Neon: Neon splits evenly about 25% of the time, argon only about 19%. More electrons means more ways to be uneven.
- Neither: electrons are always spread evenly: On average, yes. At any instant the cloud is usually a little lopsided, and that is where dispersion forces come from.

## Lab predictions: Heating Curve

CHEM 3A and 1A.

### 23. Liquid water at 0 °C freezes into ice. Which way does the heat flow?

- **✓ Out of the water: freezing is exothermic**: As the molecules lock into the lattice, attractions form and heat is released.
- Into the water: freezing is endothermic: Breaking attractions takes heat in (melting, vaporization, sublimation). Forming attractions releases heat (freezing, condensation, deposition).
- No heat flows, because the temperature stays at 0 °C: The temperature holds, but heat is still leaving: it is the energy released as attractions form.

In the lab afterward: Remove heat and watch the heat-flow label as the lattice forms.

### 24. Water is boiling at 100 °C in an open pot. The burner is turned up to high. What happens to the temperature of the boiling water?

- **✓ It stays at 100 °C, and the water boils away faster**: During a phase change the extra heat pulls more molecules apart each second. It does not make them move faster.
- It climbs above 100 °C: During a phase change the added heat breaks attractions instead of speeding the molecules up, so the temperature holds steady.
- It drops below 100 °C: Heat is flowing in, so it will not get colder. During a phase change the added heat breaks attractions instead of speeding the molecules up, so the temperature holds steady.

In the lab afterward: Add heat along the boiling step and watch the temperature readout.

## Ion–dipole

CHEM 1A.

### 28. When NaCl dissolves, each ion ends up surrounded by a shell of water molecules. What is this called?

- **✓ Hydration (solvation)**: Water molecules crowd around each ion: oxygen toward Na⁺, hydrogen toward Cl⁻. The ion–dipole attractions keep the ions apart in solution.
- Hydrogenation: Hydrogenation is a reaction that adds H₂ to a C=C double bond. Surrounding an ion with water is hydration.
- Crenation: Crenation is a red blood cell shrinking in a hypertonic solution.
- Precipitation: Precipitation is the reverse: ions leaving the solution to form a solid.

### 29. As NaCl dissolves in water, which attraction is overcome, and which one replaces it?

- **✓ The ionic bonds between Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are replaced by ion–dipole attractions to water**: Many ion–dipole attractions to water make up for the energy needed to pull the ions out of the crystal.
- Covalent bonds in NaCl are replaced by hydrogen bonds: NaCl has no covalent bonds between Na and Cl. It is held together by ionic bonds.
- Hydrogen bonds in NaCl are replaced by dispersion forces: NaCl has no hydrogen. The crystal is held together by ionic bonds.
- Nothing is overcome: the crystal just floats apart: The ions attract each other strongly. Water has to pull them apart, and ion–dipole attractions are what do it.

Hint: What holds the crystal together? What can water offer an ion?

### 30. Which liquid should dissolve the most NaCl?

- **✓ Water, H₂O**: Water is very polar, so it forms strong ion–dipole attractions with Na⁺ and Cl⁻.
- Hexane, C₆H₁₄: Hexane is nonpolar. It has no partial charges to attract the ions.
- Carbon tetrachloride, CCl₄: CCl₄ is nonpolar: its four bond dipoles cancel. It cannot pull ions out of the crystal.
- Octane, C₈H₁₈: Octane is nonpolar. It has no partial charges to attract the ions.

Hint: Which one is polar enough to attract ions?

## Sugars, starch and cellulose

CHEM 3B.

### 31. Glucose is C₆H₁₂O₆. How many O–H groups does one glucose molecule have?

- **✓ 5**: Five of the six oxygens are in O–H groups. The sixth is the ring oxygen (or the C=O of the open chain).
- 6: One of the six oxygens has no H on it: it is in the ring (or is the C=O of the open chain).
- 12: There are 12 H atoms, but seven of them are bonded to carbon. Only five are on oxygen.
- 0: Glucose is covered in O–H groups, which is why it dissolves so well in water.

### 32. Honey is mostly sugars with a little water, and it pours very slowly. What makes it so viscous?

- **✓ The sugar molecules and water are held to each other by many hydrogen bonds**: Each sugar has several O–H groups, so the molecules cling to their neighbors and resist sliding past each other.
- Covalent bonds between the sugar molecules: The sugar molecules are not bonded to each other. They are held by hydrogen bonds, which are attractions.
- The sugars are nonpolar: Sugars are very polar: every O–H group is polar.
- Dispersion forces only: Sugars have dispersion forces too, but their many O–H groups make hydrogen bonding the main attraction.

### 33. Starch and cellulose are both long chains of glucose, yet we can digest starch and not cellulose. What is different about them?

- **✓ The way the glucose units are linked: α links in starch, β links in cellulose**: The α links let starch coil, and our enzymes fit them. The β links make straight chains that pack into fibers our enzymes cannot break down.
- Cellulose has no –OH groups: Both have the same –OH groups. The difference is how the glucose units are joined.
- Cellulose is made from a different sugar: Both are made of glucose. Only the linkage differs.
- Cellulose chains are joined by disulfide bonds: Disulfide bonds are found in proteins. Carbohydrates contain no sulfur.

## Surface tension and viscosity

CHEM 3A and 1A.

### 34. Drops of water pile up into a dome on a penny before they spill over. Which property of water is this?

- **✓ Surface tension**: Water molecules at the surface are pulled inward by hydrogen bonds, so the surface acts like a stretched skin that holds the dome together.
- Viscosity: Viscosity is resistance to flowing. The dome is held by the pull at the surface.
- Vapor pressure: Vapor pressure is about molecules escaping into the gas. The dome is held by surface tension.
- Boiling point: Nothing is boiling. The dome is held by surface tension.

### 35. Water climbs up a thin glass tube, and up a paper towel, against gravity. What is this called, and why does it happen?

- **✓ Capillary action: water is attracted to the polar glass or paper, and it pulls more water along behind it**: Water hydrogen bonds to the O–H groups on glass and on paper (cellulose), and to itself, so the liquid is drawn upward.
- Osmosis: water is moving toward more solute: Osmosis is water crossing a membrane. Climbing a narrow tube is capillary action.
- Evaporation: the water turns to gas and rises: The water stays liquid as it climbs. This is capillary action.
- Viscosity: thick liquids climb: Viscosity resists flow. The climb comes from attraction to the walls of the tube.

### 36. Which liquid should have the higher surface tension: water or hexane (C₆H₁₄)?

- **✓ Water**: Water molecules hydrogen bond to each other, so the surface is hard to stretch: 72 mN/m. Hexane has dispersion forces only, and its surface tension is about a quarter of that.
- Hexane: Hexane has dispersion forces only. Weaker attractions give a lower surface tension.
- They are the same: Surface tension follows the strength of the attractions, and water’s hydrogen bonds are much stronger.
